Full Timeline

Drumming up Ideas

After the team was fresh off the heels of developing The Evil Within, a 2014 psychological,horror action game, John Johannas wanted the crew to work on something that took the team's mind off of horror games but wasn't actively thinking about what kind of creative driven game they were planning to make. John Johannas mentioned in an article by Xbox news stating that,"it was one of those ideas that you would say when drunk and then that's it, you wouldn't do anything with it." At the time of the interview, it was 10 years ago that he had somewhat of an idea but would never get to it until he managed to find time for pitching it towards the internal company.

Gauging the Key Signature

According to the article by Xbox news, John Johannas initially pitched the idea within the internal company first in order to gauge the general sentiment and it turned out that everyone seemed to be on board with the concept. While everyone liked the concept, most of the company were busy with the development of Ghostwire Tokyo and getting ready for a 2022 release date. Luckily, the only one that came forward to help Johannas fully realize the idea was a fellow programmer known as Yuji Nakamura. The only main obstacle for the pair was to pitch it in a publisher friendly way so that the idea can get passed through for the beginning stage of development.

Prototype Phase

In 2019, the pair that only consisted of John Johannas and Yuji Nakamura began the prototyping phase with developing the core concepts of the game. According to the Xbox article, Yuji Nakamura stated that "I could imagine in my mind what the game would be like, but I wasn’t really sure if I could actually make that. We had this initial concept document that John had created. And we tried to implement that into a game, or create a game prototype, of what was exactly in that concept document — that didn’t really go well. We spent about three months… two to three months… a lot of trial and error, just trying to reproduce what was in the document and it didn’t really work. And so, we had to come up with different things to try out — the fun on paper didn’t really translate well inside the prototype. That turned out to be our biggest first challenge, that we needed to make sure that the fun we had in John’s mind be present in the prototype. What happened was that when we tried to recreate what was in the document to the letter, basically what happened was it turned out to be a very slow type of action game — we needed about three or four more months to figure out how to change that to be more fun. After about the fourth month, it started to click. It worked."

Demo Showcase

After all the work they put in, they managed to put together a 15 minute demo for the likes of Shinji Mikami and the Head of Production at Bethesda, Todd Vaughn to play and received lots of great feedback. It was thanks to Todd Vaughn that the demo garnered much appraisal from the rest of the company staff that now there was internal hype for developing the game. The big challenge behind the demo had to live up to two expectations, being up to Johannas' standards and being fun for everyone. Johannas kept the development of the demo small intentionally so that they would be able to focus more on the fun aspect of the game. In this way, he was banking on people being able to resonate with the game the same way he was able to enjoy it.

Assembling the Team

The best part about the team that was formed around this game were essentially people that were available at the time. John Johannas quotes in the Xbox article stating that,"All the people who were available had literally no experience making a game like this." The fact that John Johannas did not need to hand pick people to aid development of the game left a challenging air of creativity to be developed throughout the employees. Some of the employees that were developing Ghostwire Tokyo as well helped out with the development of Hi-Fi Rush on their own time. Audio Director Shuichi Nobori stated that "John and Nakamura-san were [the only] two people working on it and everybody else in the studio was working on Ghostwire: Tokyo, myself included. I was asked to help on it when I had time available, kind of like a part-time thing; to help when you can. But as we started seeing John and Nakamura-san iterating on their work, and being very creative, and seeing how they were improving on what they’d been creating, people tended to gravitate toward that creative excitement. The strength of the creativity there helped bring in a lot of people." The key to success in aligning the employees to Johannas' and Nakamura's vision was the prototype that they developed. Playing the demo was key for others to reach a common understanding of what music to blend with the engaging world of Hi-Fi Rush. Keita Sakai, one of the Lead Artists at Tango Gameworks, doubted the idea of blending rhythm and action because they ought to rather go for one core genre to focus on but Johannas was insistent on their vision to blend the two together. The Lead Art Director Keita Sakai stated in the Xbox interview stating that "The core of the game is rhythm action. But as I talked to John initially — and John was very passionate about the rhythm action portion of the game — I was saying stuff like, “No, you must choose between rhythm or action. You can’t have both.” John kept saying, “It’s not one or the other, both are important,” and everybody said, “You must choose one,” and he’s like, “No, no, no, no, no, no.” It took a while before we were able to understand what he was really going after, but once we saw it, it dawned on us that John’s sensibilities to capture that so early on, in something that might be fun, was pretty amazing."

Full Blown Development

Initially the game was going to be a small project but because many people were onboard with the concept of the game, the budget blew past a typical AA game. Johannas stated that "It was supposed to be a small project from Tango. And people probably see it as this weird, sort-of AA title. Or people are like, “Oh, they made a nice indie game.” This ain’t no indie game. Obviously, I can’t say how much it cost, but it was not a cheap game to make." Everyone was also bouncing around between different roles until there were more people aboard the Hi-Fi Rush train for Johannas to take the backseat of the development. His role focused more on lead level design and guiding how each of the levels will play out. Johannas had to make sure that everyone else was on the same boat with splitting the action and rhythm aspects 70 to 30. Many of the newer additions to the team wanted to introduce their own takes of the game but Johannas had to make sure everyone else was aligned with his vision. It was a balancing act of guiding the team to the right direction and trial and error, but ultimately with all the time they were given, they were able to get the game out with much polish and sheen to the name Hi-Fi Rush.

The Announcement that lurked behind the Shadows

Johannas had found a tough time announcing the game because he knew it would get absolutely drowned out by the hard hitting Christmas releases but he made the final decision to shadow drop it during the Xbox Developers Direct that led so many of us to be amazed(including myself) to want to play this game, and really experience how rhythm and action can be played and complimented in each others strengths. What became of it was a standout hit that really hit the nail of what made a game fun to play. The story was quite engaging with the depth given to the characters and the plot but it ultimately made me feel like I was enjoying a saturday morning cartoon that kept my engagement every step of the way.

Where they are Now

While Hifi-Rush made a big splash onto the scenes, it seemed that Xbox did not want to fully embrace the release and praise of the games achievements. Immediately a year after Hi-Fi Rush came out, Xbox got rid of the studio in favor of upholding its bottom line. Later on, Krafton, a company known for developing PUBG, offered to buy Tango Gameworks and saved the company from dying out. Succeeding in the buyout, Hi-Fi Rush is now available under their name. There is plans for Hi-Fi Rush 2 but no concrete information was given as of Today.
